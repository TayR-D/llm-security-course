The first layer of the stack (M10) lives inside the model itself, put there during training
(M3). These safeguards travel with the weights — which is their strength (always on, no extra
latency) and their weakness (can't be updated without retraining, and can be fine-tuned away
on open weights).

- **Refusal training** (SFT + RLHF/RLAIF): teaches the model to decline disallowed requests.
- **Constitutional AI**: uses a written constitution and AI feedback instead of human labels
  for every example, so alignment scales.
- **Specs / constitutions as the policy source**: the explicit document the model (and often
  the runtime classifiers, M12) is optimized against.
- **Safe completions vs hard refusals**: answering the safe part of a request instead of a
  blanket "I can't help with that" — less over-refusal (M12), same safety.
- **Instruction-hierarchy training**: teaching the model to rank instructions by privilege so
  a user — or an injection — can't override the system.
- **Adversarial training & tamper resistance**: training against known attacks, and research
  into making open-weight alignment harder to strip out.

## Why training-time alone isn't enough

Everything here is a *trained tendency*, not a guarantee (M3) — which is exactly why the stack
adds runtime layers on top (M12–M14). Two limits in particular:

- **It can't be patched quickly.** A newly discovered jailbreak can't wait for the next
  training run; a runtime classifier can be updated today.
- **It doesn't survive open weights.** Fine-tuning can remove refusal (M7, M9), and no
  training-time safeguard is present at inference to stop that. This is central to your
  self-hosted capstone, where you run an open-weight model with refusals removed.
