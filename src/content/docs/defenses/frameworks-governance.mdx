Zoom all the way out. Which safeguards a model *must* have before release isn't decided
per-feature — it's decided by a **frontier safety framework**: a policy that ties capability
thresholds to required protections. Reach a threshold, and stronger security and deployment
safeguards become mandatory.

All four major labs now run one, and they rhyme even though the names differ. Compare the rungs:

## The shared shape, and why it matters

Each framework has a baseline, a "serious uplift" rung that triggers hardened safeguards, and a
"catastrophic" rung that can pause a release. The common pattern — *if a model reaches
capability X, then safeguards Y become required* — is what makes these auditable rather than
aspirational. It also explains the product decisions you saw earlier: Mythos-class access, the
reroute-to-Opus behavior, and Flash Cyber's gating all fall out of where a model lands on its
lab's ladder.

## Voluntary vs legally binding

A 2026 wrinkle worth knowing: several labs now keep *two* documents. Anthropic's **Responsible
Scaling Policy** is its voluntary framework, while a separate **Frontier Compliance Framework**
is the one designated under laws like California's SB 53; OpenAI similarly published a Frontier
Governance Framework as its statutory document
([RSP v3, Feb 2026](https://anthropic.com/news/responsible-scaling-policy-v3)). The legal driver
matters: the EU Code of Practice, SB 53, and New York's RAISE Act are increasingly what *require*
a framework to exist at all.

## External checks

Frameworks are stronger when someone outside the lab tests the model: the UK AI Security
Institute and the US Center for AI Standards and Innovation (CAISI) run independent evaluations,
and labs commission external red-teaming (e.g. Gray Swan) — all of which feed the system cards
and risk reports that the comparison matrix (M17) cites.
