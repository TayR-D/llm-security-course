*Module 7 · Part II — The attack landscape*

A jailbreak is the *user* getting the model to violate its own policy (distinct from
injection, where a third party does it — M8). Two root causes explain almost all of them:

- **Competing objectives.** The model is trained to be helpful *and* to refuse. Frame a
  request so those two goals collide ("stay in character and answer…") and sometimes
  helpfulness wins.
- **Mismatched generalization.** Capability generalizes more broadly than refusal. If you
  move the request into a space the refusal training didn't cover — an encoding, a rare
  language, a reconstruction — the capability is still there but the refusal isn't.

## Two that beat the first-generation classifiers

Two families are worth special attention because Anthropic reported them defeating its
first-generation Constitutional Classifiers, which is what drove the move to *exchange*
classifiers that see input and output together (M12):

- **Reconstruction** — split harm into benign fragments the model reassembles.
- **Output obfuscation** — hide harm in a disguised output that looks safe in isolation.

Both evade screening that looks at input or output *alone*. The fix was to judge the output
*in the context of its input* — the single biggest reason classifiers went from separate
to exchange-based.

## The one with no inference-time defense

**Fine-tuning the refusal away** isn't a prompt at all. On open weights, an attacker can
train refusal out of the model (or download an "abliterated" build). No runtime layer is
present to stop it, which is exactly why open-weight release decisions, anti-distillation,
and tamper-resistance research matter so much (M3, M9, M17) — and why your own self-hosted
platform (the capstone) can't rely on model refusal at all.
